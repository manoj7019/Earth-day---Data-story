Our Power Already Exists

India’s organic waste streams could yield up to 86 BCM/yr (62 million metric tonnes (MMT)) of biomethane annually. This means it replaces 12 years of fuel for every CNG vehicle in India, without a single additional import, and 170 billion litres of diesel.





This is enough to meet India’s entire natural gas demand and still have roughly a third left over. Today, around 4% of that potential is being captured across domestic and industrial spaces, while India spends $14.9 billion annually on LNG imports.

Germany is currently the largest biogas producer, producing 87 TWh per year. China ranks second in biogas production, with 81 TWh. India currently produces 4.7 TWh* of biogas, which is is about 5.4% of Germany's 87 TWh and 5.8% of China's 81 TWh.



India’ sustainable CBG potential (62 MMT) = 540TWh of gross biogas energy (using a typical 6 kWh per cubic metre conversion factor). This scale reflects not just geology or geography, but the amount of organic residue, waste and manure from our farms and cities. The power has always been here. The question is how fast it can be organised.

The Map is Already Drawn

India’s biogas opportunity is no longer limited to small or decentralised systems. Large-scale compressed biogas (CBG) plants are already being built across states. Uttar Pradesh leads with the highest installed capacity, followed by Maharashtra and Madhya Pradesh — forming the core of the current biogas market. A second layer of activity is visible across Punjab, Haryana, and Gujarat — regions where agricultural intensity and residue availability are high.

Our Power. Our Planet.

Converting agricultural residues into compressed biogas (CBG) offers significant dual benefits as a clean energy investment.
The first is energy. A 5 TPD CBG plant alone does more than generate fuel. Replacing conventional fuels can reduce over 4,700 tonnes of CO₂-equivalent emissions every year — the equivalent of displacing more than 2,000 tonnes of gasoline.



It also replaces synthetic fertilisers — reducing both input costs and emissions. In one year, this can offset over 300 tonnes of CO₂-equivalent emissions from the agriculture system. But the largest impact comes from what doesn’t happen. When organic waste is diverted from landfills and processed instead, it can prevent up to 37,500 tonnes of CO₂-equivalent emissions annually.



The second is soil. Every CBG plant produces a co-product alongside its gas: fermented organic manure, a digestate that rebuilds soil organic carbon. A major ICAR-coordinated survey of 254,236 soil samples across 620 Indian districts found that soil organic carbon is declining across large parts of the country. This is not a marginal improvement. It is a system-level shift — driven by a single unit of infrastructure.



The Power Isn’t Coming. It’s Already Here

It is in the fields of Uttar Pradesh. The crop cycles of Punjab and Haryana. In the waste generated across India’s cities. It always has been. The shift is not discovery. It is a conversion. Converting waste into fuel. Converting emissions into energy. Converting a distributed resource into an organised infrastructure. This requires systems, collection, processing, distribution, and markets that make it viable at scale. None of it is new. But all of it needs to be built. And it is being built — plant by plant, tonne by tonne.
